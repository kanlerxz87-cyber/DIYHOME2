Wallpaper tools

Do you actually need a wallpaper tool kit for peel-and-stick?

Most peel-and-stick jobs fail in the first ten minutes, and almost never because of the paper. They fail because of the tool — or the lack of one.

Published 2026-11-206 min readReviewed against official sources

What this guide is

A buying guide, not a review. It explains what the tools in a starter kit do, when a kit is the wrong purchase, and the installation order that keeps bubbles out. It does not claim any product performs better than another.

The short answer

If you are hanging peel-and-stick wallpaper, contact paper or a vinyl backsplash for the first time, a basic kit is usually worth more than the extra roll of paper you would otherwise waste. If you already own a plastic squeegee from a screen protector, a sharp craft knife and a tape measure, you own most of a kit already and can skip it.

What a kit buys you is not performance. It buys consistency: the right edge, the right blade, and a measuring tape that is actually in the drawer when you start instead of at the back of a car.

What is actually in a starter kit

The three tools that matter are all unglamorous. A broad smoothing tool presses air out ahead of the adhesive without dragging the surface. A squeegee with a felt or plastic edge does the same on smaller panels. A utility knife with a fresh blade cuts a clean trim line at the ceiling and the baseboard, which is where the eye goes first.

The kit on this site's register contains a large smoothing tool, a blue squeegee, a utility knife with replacement blades, and a measuring tape. That is a complete set for a first job and an incomplete set for a large one.

What is usually missing from kits: a seam roller for joins between drops, a level long enough to draw a true vertical line, and a sponge. None of those are optional on a full wall.

When a kit is the wrong purchase

Three cases. First, if the job is a single shelf or a strip behind a cooker, a card wrapped in a cloth does the same work as a smoothing tool. Second, if you already own the three tools, a kit duplicates them. Third, if you are hanging a large-format or heavy-gauge paper, the smoothing tool in a basic kit is often narrower than you want and you will spend the job chasing creases.

The honest framing: a starter kit removes the excuse not to start. It does not make a difficult paper easy.

The order of operations that keeps bubbles out

Bubbles are mostly geometry. Air trapped behind a panel has nowhere to go, so the only reliable fix is to never trap it: peel a little, press a little, work from the centre outward, and keep the backing paper between your tool and the surface on the first pass.

This sequence is the standard one and needs no proprietary tools:

  • Clean and dry the surface completely. Adhesive does not stick to dust, and it does not stick to grease at all.
  • Measure the height of the drop twice and cut once, with a generous margin at both ends. Trimming is easy; adding is not.
  • Draw a true vertical line with a level and align the first drop to it, not to the corner of the room, which is rarely square.
  • Peel back roughly thirty centimetres of backing at a time and press from the centre outward with the smoothing tool.
  • Push — do not drag — the tool. Dragging stretches the paper and the stretch comes back as a wrinkle a day later.
  • Trim the top and bottom last, using a fresh blade against a straight edge, then roll the seams.
  • Re-press the seams after an hour. Adhesive creeps as it warms.

It also says nothing about how a particular paper behaves on a particular wall. Texture, paint sheen and room temperature all change the answer, and none of them can be checked from a listing.
